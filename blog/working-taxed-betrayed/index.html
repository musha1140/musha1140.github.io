Working, taxed, and betrayed

Working, Taxed, and Betrayed: How America’s Labor Majority Is Funding Its Own Decline

For all the slogans about “hard-working Americans,” the truth is far uglier: the American worker is not just underpaid and overworked. They are being methodically fleeced to fund the very machine that crushes them. The FY2025 House Budget and Trump-aligned tax proposals are a masterpiece of misdirection: a legislative magic trick designed to offer crumbs to the working class while funneling vast sums upward to the ultra-wealthy and entrenched interests.

The blueprint for this economic betrayal isn’t new, it’s a long-standing architecture of power that dates back to the very construction of America’s modern institutions. In 1935, when the Social Security Act was passed, it carved out a convenient exception: domestic and agricultural workers, jobs disproportionately held by Black and Latino laborers, were excluded. That was structural racism carried out through bureaucratic design. From the beginning, labor protections were filtered through a lens of who was “worthy.”

Fast forward to 1954, and the federal government formalizes its exclusionary practices into full-blown militarized deportation. Under Dwight D. Eisenhower, “Operation Wetback” authorized mass roundups of over a million Mexicans and Mexican-Americans. It wasn’t just an immigration policy, it was a demonstration of force against racialized labor, ensuring economic compliance through fear. At the same time, this domestic agenda of racial sorting was paired with international suppression: the CIA executed a coup in Guatemala to oust President Jacobo Árbenz, whose land reforms threatened U.S. agribusiness monopolies like the United Fruit Company. Framed as anti-communist, it was pure economic protectionism masquerading as global freedom.

The same pattern had already played out the previous year in Iran. In 1953, the CIA and British intelligence launched Operation Ajax to remove the democratically elected Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh, who had nationalized Iran’s oil industry. In his place, they reinstalled the Shah, a monarch who ruled with an iron fist for decades. This single operation laid the groundwork for decades of resentment, culminating in the 1979 Islamic Revolution. The American-backed repression and exploitation under the Shah stoked anti-Western fervor, leading to the hostage crisis and a rupture in U.S.-Iran relations that persists to this day.

These dual tracks, domestic repression and foreign intervention, would come to define U.S. policy through the Cold War and beyond. When Congo’s Patrice Lumumba leaned toward sovereignty, he was eliminated with Western help. When Chile’s Salvador Allende pursued socialism through democracy, he was bombed in his palace, and Augusto Pinochet’s military dictatorship took over, with CIA backing. When civil rights leaders at home like Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X called for racial and economic justice, Hoover’s FBI turned its domestic surveillance into an anti-Negro army under COINTELPRO, infiltrating, disrupting, and assassinating.

None of this was coincidence. It was a logic. Any attempt, foreign or domestic, to challenge the centralized control of wealth and capital was reclassified as a threat to national security. The pretext of ideological war enabled a material war against autonomy, whether that autonomy belonged to Black sharecroppers in Mississippi or socialists in Santiago.

By the 1980s, this logic extended to Grenada. The Reagan administration justified an invasion under the pretense of protecting American medical students. In reality, it was a show of force against a left-leaning government aligning with Cuba. Once again, local sovereignty was steamrolled by the machinery of geopolitical economics.

The Reagan-era alliances and covert operations in Afghanistan would prove even more catastrophic. The U.S., through Operation Cyclone, funneled billions in weapons and funding to mujahideen fighters to bleed the Soviet Union. Among those who benefitted from this program was Osama bin Laden and the very network that would become al-Qaeda. What was seen as a strategic Cold War move ultimately metastasized into the September 11 attacks, terrorism born from a proxy war that Washington helped build.

By 2000, this logic had evolved into financial warfare. Saddam Hussein didn’t have weapons of mass destruction, but he did threaten to price oil in euros, which would destabilize the petrodollar, the backbone of U.S. economic dominance. Iraq was pulverized not to liberate Iraqis but to defend the supremacy of a currency backed by trust and military might. The cost? Trillions in taxpayer dollars. The source? Your paycheck.

Iran’s inclusion in BRICS was equally intolerable to U.S. hegemony. With Tehran’s support for an alternative energy trade system and growing alignment with Russia and China, the nation represented a direct challenge to Western economic primacy. Instead of diplomacy, the U.S. imposed sanctions, funded covert operations, and maintained a shadow military presence in the region. It wasn’t about nukes. It was about dollars, and the threat of trading oil in anything else.

While bombs dropped abroad, workers at home paid payroll taxes into the Social Security Trust Fund, trusting it would be there for them later. But those funds were borrowed, converted into Treasury IOUs, and rerouted to fund operations the public never voted for. Every bomb, every drone, every contractor paycheck came with a silent invoice addressed to the American worker.

By 2015, figures like Timothy Mellon emerged, not as new actors, but as heirs to the American economic caste system. In his own words, welfare programs were “Slavery Redux.” His solution? Fund the rollback. He poured over $100 million into Trump-aligned PACs and RFK Jr.’s campaign, not to support reform, but to destroy redistribution mechanisms. His money ensures a government that speaks the language of bootstrap virtue while gutting public supports for the poor.

So when Trump returns in 2025 with a “no income tax on tips” promise, it isn’t a gift to workers. It’s a distraction. Servers may save $4,000, but they lose twice that in health and food aid. Medicaid is slashed by $880 billion. SNAP is cut by $230 billion. The child nutrition and education budgets are gutted. This isn’t fiscal responsibility, it’s policy sabotage cloaked in populist rhetoric. The tax break isn’t relief. It’s a sleight-of-hand to justify the collapse of a social safety net.

Meanwhile, Trump receives his $5.3 billion Air Force One, a jet wrapped in gold trim, tailored for a man obsessed with spectacle. Built with borrowed funds from the Social Security Trust, this aircraft becomes a flying monument to elite self-indulgence, funded, ironically, by the very workers being told their entitlements are unsustainable.

BRICS, a growing coalition including Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Egypt, Iran, and the UAE, attempts to shift global trade away from the U.S. dollar. Their rebellion isn’t ideological. It’s practical. But the response is predictable. U.S. troops appear in or alongside every BRICS or BRICS-adjacent country under the guise of joint exercises or anti-terror operations:

Brazil hosts Operation Formosa. India runs Yudh Abhyas drills. South Africa hosts African Lion. UAE houses Al Dhafra Air Base. Saudi Arabia gets Prince Sultan. Iraq, still occupied, continues Operation Inherent Resolve. Djibouti remains home to Camp Lemonnier. Iran, while not housing U.S. troops, is ringed by military installations in Bahrain, Qatar, and historically in Afghanistan, reflecting the same policy of perimeter encirclement used during the Cold War.

And in tandem with military deployment, Trump tours these countries with economic deals in hand, investment carrots backed by military sticks. This isn’t diplomacy. It’s global asset protection. This isn’t policy. It’s enforcement.

Back home, tax policy mirrors these same imperial strategies. The “no tax on tips” provision empowers business owners operating in cash-heavy economies, like Trump’s own golf courses. These LLCs are designed to be financial black holes: they split revenue streams into hundreds of fragments across subsidiaries, burying profits under layers of debt and depreciation. The IRS, already underfunded, cannot pierce these veils. In the 2012 case against John Kennedy, a real estate mogul running golf resorts similar to Trump’s, prosecutors revealed how these structures functioned to both underpay taxes and overinflate business expenses. The system doesn’t fail, it’s exploited exactly as intended.

And then there’s Hoover, whose legacy never left. The modern IRS targets gig workers. Federal surveillance zeroes in on activists. Police budgets balloon while libraries close. COINTELPRO didn’t die, it got digitized.

Nothing about the FY2025 budget is about fiscal prudence. It’s about reallocating obligation. The worker gets tax exemptions on pennies and pays for billions in weapons. The poor get lectures on discipline while their labor funds a security state and gilded jets.

The American worker is footing the bill for their own marginalization. They are the labor, the revenue stream, the justification for austerity, and the casualty of policy.*

*As of the latest 2025 developments, the administration has signaled intent to revisit mass deportation frameworks modeled after Eisenhower’s 1954 “Operation Wetback.” Legal scholars and civil liberties watchdogs have raised alarms that Trump’s executive orders may include suspension or circumvention of habeas corpus protections for non-citizens, a constitutional right already eroded under previous administrations via national security justifications. The deployment of ICE raids combined with expanded expedited removal zones signals a revival of racialized, large-scale deportation tactics under a modernized legal guise.

This isn’t just about domestic policy. The U.S. has entered into a $6 million agreement with El Salvador to deport alleged gang members, primarily Venezuelans, to the Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo (CECOT), a mega-prison infamous for its brutal, dehumanizing conditions. Many of the deportees have not been convicted of any crimes in the U.S., yet they are being transferred into indefinite detention, denied legal counsel, and subjected to forced labor. Inside CECOT, prisoners are routinely made to build furniture, assemble parts, and produce state-sold goods under extreme confinement and psychological pressure. International human rights groups have labeled these conditions tantamount to modern slavery, with detainees held in overcrowded, windowless cells for months without sunlight or recreation.

In a move reminiscent of past overreaches, the administration’s reliance on CECOT raises disturbing parallels with Cold War-era extradition and extraordinary rendition programs. It reflects an escalating strategy to bypass constitutional constraints while exporting incarceration for political gain.

The American worker, therefore, is not only funding domestic policies that undermine their well-being but also international agreements that compromise human rights and legal protections. This is not broken government; this is how it was always meant to function.
