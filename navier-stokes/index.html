Millennium Prize problem

Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness

The Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem asks whether the equations that govern an incompressible, viscous fluid in three dimensions always have smooth solutions that exist for all time, or whether a flow that starts smooth can develop a singularity. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems chosen by the Clay Mathematics Institute.

Two settings

The problem is posed in two settings. The first is ordinary Euclidean space ℝ³, which needs extra conditions on how the initial condition and the solution behave far away. The second removes the problems at infinity by making everything periodic, so the equations live on the three-dimensional torus 𝕋³ = ℝ³/ℤ³ instead of on ℝ³.

In Euclidean space ℝ³

The initial velocity v₀(x) is smooth and divergence-free, and it decays faster than any power of the distance: for every multi-index α and every K > 0 there is a constant c = c(α, K) > 0 such that

|∂^α v₀(x)| ≤ c / (1 + |x|)^K          for all x ∈ ℝ³

Depending on the version of the problem, the external force f(x, t) is smooth, real analytic, or zero, and satisfies the same kind of bound, now with time derivatives included:

|∂^α f(x, t)| ≤ c / (1 + |x| + t)^K    for all (x, t) ∈ ℝ³ × [0, ∞)

A physically reasonable solution is required to meet two conditions:

  1. The velocity and pressure are smooth everywhere and for all time: v(x, t) ∈ C^∞(ℝ³ × [0, ∞)), p(x, t) ∈ C^∞(ℝ³ × [0, ∞))
  2. The kinetic energy stays bounded: there is a constant E ∈ (0, ∞) with ∫_ℝ³ |v(x, t)|² dx < E for all t ≥ 0

Existence and smoothness in ℝ³. For every initial condition v₀(x) that is smooth, divergence-free and decaying as above, with external force f(x, t) = 0, there exist a velocity field v(x, t) and a pressure p(x, t) that solve the Navier–Stokes equations and satisfy conditions 1 and 2.

Breakdown in ℝ³. There exist an initial condition v₀(x) and an external force f(x, t) with the required smoothness, analyticity or zero value, for which no solution satisfying conditions 1 and 2 exists.

On the torus 𝕋³

Here the velocity is periodic in each space variable with period 1. With unit vectors e₁ = (1, 0, 0), e₂ = (0, 1, 0), e₃ = (0, 0, 1), periodicity means

v(x + eᵢ, t) = v(x, t)    for i = 1, 2, 3 and all (x, t) ∈ ℝ³ × [0, ∞)

Taking coordinates mod 1 turns ℝ³ into the torus

𝕋³ = { (θ₁, θ₂, θ₃) : 0 ≤ θᵢ < 1, i = 1, 2, 3 }

The initial velocity is smooth and divergence-free, and the external force is smooth, real analytic or zero. The two conditions become:

  1. v(x, t) ∈ C^∞(𝕋³ × [0, ∞)), p(x, t) ∈ C^∞(𝕋³ × [0, ∞))
  2. ∫_𝕋³ |v(x, t)|² dx < E for all t ≥ 0

Existence and smoothness on 𝕋³. For every smooth, divergence-free, spatially periodic initial velocity v₀(x), with f(x, t) = 0, there exist smooth, globally defined solutions on 𝕋³ satisfying conditions 3 and 4.

Breakdown on 𝕋³. There exist an initial condition and an external force on 𝕋³ for which no solution satisfying conditions 3 and 4 exists.

The four Millennium statements

The prize is awarded for proving any one of four statements:

Statement Setting Claim External force
(A) ℝ³ smooth solutions always exist zero
(B) 𝕋³ smooth solutions always exist zero
(C) ℝ³ breakdown: some smooth start has no smooth solution smooth force allowed
(D) 𝕋³ breakdown: some smooth start has no smooth solution smooth force allowed

Because the existence statements require zero force while the breakdown statements allow a smooth force, (A) is not the negation of (C), and (B) is not the negation of (D). Only these implications hold:

¬(A) ⟹ (C)     ¬(C) ⟹ (A)     ¬(B) ⟹ (D)     ¬(D) ⟹ (B)

None of the reverse implications hold. So the prize problem can be counted as solved while one or more of the four statements remains open. Other versions exist outside the prize, such as the problem with a real analytic external force.

Results before 2026

1934, Jean Leray. Leray proved that smooth, globally defined solutions exist when the initial velocity v₀(x) is small enough. He also proved that weak solutions exist: functions that satisfy the equations in an averaged sense rather than point by point.

1960s. The finite difference method was proven to converge for the Navier–Stokes equations, the equations were solved numerically, and smooth global solutions were proven to exist in two dimensions (Ladyzhenskaya).

Blowup time. For a given initial velocity v₀(x) there is a maximum time T, the blowup time, for which smooth solutions exist on ℝ³ × (0, T). Either statement (A) or (B) is true, or T is finite for some v₀(x).

2016: Terence Tao's averaged equation

In "Finite time blowup for an averaged three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equation," Terence Tao formalized a "supercriticality barrier" for the global regularity problem and suggested that his method hints at a route to blowup for the true equations.

With viscosity ν rescaled to 1 and f = 0, a smooth divergence-free solution is equivalent to a "mild" solution v : [0, ∞) → H¹⁰_df(ℝ³), where Hᵏ(Ω) = W^(k,2)(Ω) ⊂ L²(Ω) is the Sobolev space of square-integrable functions whose first k partial derivatives are also square-integrable. The Euler bilinear form is

B(u, v) = −½ L((u·∇)v + (v·∇)u),     with ⟨B(u, u), u⟩ = 0

where L is the Leray projection onto divergence-free fields, Luᵢ = uᵢ − Δ⁻¹ ∂ᵢ∂ⱼ uⱼ. With the normalized pressure p = −Δ⁻¹ ∂ᵢ∂ⱼ(uᵢuⱼ), the pressure drops out of the equations:

∂u/∂t = Δu + B(u, u)

Tao replaced B with an averaged form B̃ that keeps the cancellation ⟨B̃(u, u), u⟩ = 0 but has weaker properties, and showed that for this averaged equation there is an initial velocity with no global mild solution. Any successful approach to the real problem therefore has to use finer structure of the true Euler bilinear form B than the averaged version has.

September 2026: the OpenAI blowup claim

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI stated that it had solved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem with an internal frontier language model, showing initial conditions that lead to finite time blowup. It released a paper and a Lean formalization. As reported, the work used a swarm of about 10,000 AI agents, partly orchestrated with the OpenAI Codex tool, producing roughly 4.9 million messages and 300 billion output tokens, about 2.7 million messages and 130 billion tokens of it on Navier–Stokes. The claimed solution was reached on 5 September after 88 hours, at a cost of millions of dollars in compute and electricity.

On 6 October 2026, a paper on arXiv found that the Lean proof did not faithfully represent the natural-language proof.

Priority dispute

Shortly before the announcement, Tristan Buckmaster (New York University) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) had reported advances on the Euler equations. Buckmaster said OpenAI adopted their methods; OpenAI denied this and said its model solved the Euler problem independently, by different methods. OpenAI researcher Sébastien Bubeck wrote that the work began partly after vague posts by Alpöge. Buckmaster suggested his Codex usage could have become training data; on 10 September OpenAI said an investigation confirmed his prompts could not have influenced the system.

Construction outline

The construction is implicit. As of 18 September 2026 the proof of statement (C) had not been independently verified. The solution resembles a spinning top that gets thinner and shorter, producing a velocity singularity at t = 1 near the origin:

lim_{t→1⁻} ‖v‖_{L^∞(ℝ³)} = ∞,      while ‖v‖_{L²(ℝ³)} < ∞ at t = 1

In cylindrical coordinates (r, θ, z), independent of the angle θ, with time to singularity τ = 1 − t and a parameter 0 < h < 1/100:

characteristic radius   ℓ_r ~ τ^(1/2)
characteristic height   ℓ_z ~ τ^(1/2 − h)        so  ℓ_r / ℓ_z ~ τ^h
average velocities      |u_θ|, |u_z| ~ τ^(−1/2 − h),   |u_r| ∈ O(τ^(−1/2))
volume                  V ~ ℓ_r² ℓ_z ~ τ^(3/2 − h)
velocity squared        |u|² ~ τ^(−1 − 2h)
kinetic energy          T ~ |u|² V ~ τ^(1/2 − 3h)  → 0 as τ → 0⁺

The velocities diverge while the kinetic energy goes to zero. The paper then changes variables, with A = ½ + h and D = ½ − h:

τ = q(1 − η²),   z = q^D η,   X = r² / (2q)
r u_r = V₀,   r u_θ = q^(−A) E,   r u_z = q^(−A) U,   p = q^(−2A) Π

with regularity on the axis and radial pressure balance

V₀(0, η) = 0,        Π(X, η) = ∫_X^∞ E(x, η) / (2x) dx

The force-free equations are written as a residual set to zero:

R(u, p) = ∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u − Δu + ∇p

The construction runs in reverse: pick smooth u and p on ℝ³ × [0, 1), each with compact support in a compact set K, and define the force as f = R(u, p). For every multi-index α and every j, ∂ₓ^α ∂ₜ^j f converges uniformly as t → 1⁻ to ∂ₓ^α Fⱼ(x) for smooth compactly supported Fⱼ with ∂ₓ^α Fⱼ(0) = 0, which lets f be extended smoothly onto ℝ³ × [0, ∞) with support in K × [0, 2].

The unforced case

On 17 September 2026, Peter Constantin, Mihaela Ignatova and Vlad Vicol released a preprint arguing that the method does not extend to a real analytic external force. Since zero force is real analytic, the method does not reach statements (A) and (B).

In popular culture

The problem appears in the novel The Mathematician's Shiva (2014), whose fictional mathematician takes her proof to the grave, and the film Gifted (2017) refers to the Millennium Prize problems, Navier–Stokes among them.
